Pareto Atlas

You can't flatten a sphere without distortion, so the "best" world map is a three-way tug-of-war: preserve shapes, areas, or distances — never all three. Instead of picking a favorite compromise, we let a machine hill-climb the entire trade-off surface. One discovered map, Vantage, beats the Winkel Tripel on all three at once; another sets a new record for distance fidelity.

04 — How the machine finds a map

Autoresearch, in four moves

01

Parametrize

A projection is two symmetric polynomials in lon/lat — 18 free numbers. No named projection sits exactly on any coefficient vector.

02

Measure ×3

Shape = ⟨ln(a/b)²⟩ and area = Var(ln a·b) from Tissot's indicatrix (local); distance = Var(ln of map-distance ÷ true great-circle distance) over thousands of point pairs (global).

03

Hill-climb

Adam gradient descent minimizes a weighted blend of the three, with a barrier that forbids the map from folding.

04

Sweep the surface

Walking the weight simplex traces the whole 3-objective Pareto surface — the triangle above is that surface, made draggable.

The three-way trade-off

Each corner is a pure optimum; every real map lives inside. The classics are single hand-picked points — autoresearch maps the entire interior and finds the dominant ones.
